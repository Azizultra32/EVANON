## Paper Summary

### SUMMARY PASSAGE 1

### Introduction

In Western scholarship, claims made for Zoroastrian monotheism or dualism are linked to the premise of "Zarathustra's reform," according to which Zarathustra rejected the inherited Indo-Iranian beliefs and preached a reformed religion as reflected in his five GĀÎ,Ās 'songs', the oldest part of the Avesta. Studies have therefore tended to focus on the precise nature of Zarathustra's reform and the extent to which later religious beliefs remained true to "Zarathustra's own teachings." This, in turn, has encouraged the notion that there is little need for studying this religion or its literature as part of organically evolving traditions, and nonspecialists writing about or referring to Zoroastrianism often do so, uncritically, on the basis of the sometimes rather diverging descriptions of Zarathustra and his reform in 20th-century secondary literature.

### SUMMARY PASSAGE 2

### The Zoroastrian Literature

Two linguistic forms are present, one older and one younger, referred to as Old and Young(er) Avestan; hence, the Avesta is divided into the Old and Young(er) Avesta. The Old Avesta contains the five GĀÎÄs, the literary authorship of which (since Haug) is traditionally ascribed to Zarathustra in the West; and the Yasna HaptanghÄiti, a hymn in praise of Ahura MazdÄ and his creations. Among Young Avestan texts that I shall mention here are the Yasna, the text accompanying the morning ritual (see below); the Yashts, which are hymns to individual deities; and the Videvdad 'the rules for keeping the daÄ“was [bad old gods] away', which contains rules about pollution and purification.

### SUMMARY PASSAGE 3

### Zarathustra, Historical Prophet And Reformer?

Contra Haug, F. Spiegel pointed out that Zarathustra's vita was a legend, and what we see in the GĀÎÄs is exactly what we see in the rest of the Avesta. The effect of Haug's limitation of Zarathustra's responsibility for the Avesta to a tiny part of it was to disassociate him from the rituals described in the clearly polytheistic Yasna and Videvdad, which seemed completely heathen to Western sensibilities. In this way, the obvious cosmological dualism in Zoroastrianism-which in the 18th and 19th centuries still, no doubt, smacked strongly of heresy-was finessed.

### SUMMARY PASSAGE 4

### The Scholarly Basis For Opinions On Zarathustra

Haug's and Bartholomae's conclusions were therefore based on largely circular arguments but were avidly embraced by Western Iranists, who were now free to start constructing their own Zarathustras. From the end of the 19th century and throughout the 20th century, arguments for Zarathustra's historicity were then also produced, the most common being the vivid and personal Zarathustra image in the GĀÎÄs. From a modern point of view, the vividness of literary characters does not prove, of course, that they were real people, yet the argument was still wielded at the end of the 20th century, as illustrated by M. Boyce's "recognizably real figure of the Gathas" (see below).

### SUMMARY PASSAGE 5

### Zarathustra In The Texts

In the Young Avesta, Zarathustra battles the Dark (or: Evil) Spirit and his minions, thus contributing to placing Ahura MazdÄ back in command of the world. He is also repeatedly presented as the first human to reject the daÄ“was, the (bad) old gods, and to praise Ahura MazdÄ's order and sacrifice to him (Yasht 13.89). Thereby, he would drive the old gods and even the Dark Spirit himself back underground and into Hell (Yasna 9.14-15, Yasht 17.18-20, Videvdad 19).

### SUMMARY PASSAGE 6

### Life Without Zarathustra

The Avesta is fundamentally ahistorical, located in mythical times and places, and all mentions of Zarathustra in the Young Avesta and the Pahlavi Books are of the same kind: Zarathustra's locus is the Aryan Territory-that is, the mythical homeland of the Iranians, thought to have been located in the central continent of the earth, surrounded by the six others. In fact, the Avesta and the Pahlavi texts contain no nonmythological, "historicizing" Zarathustra narrative.

### SUMMARY PASSAGE 8

### The Yasna As Reordering And Re-Creation Ritual And The Saoshyant

In the Young Avesta, the term saoshyant is applied to Zarathustra's three eschatological sons, who, according to the Pahlavi texts, will be born at thousand-year intervals after Zarathustra and, by their sacrifices, will bring the world closer to perfection. The last, the saoshyant par excellence (Pahlavi SÅshÄns) is described in Yashts 13 and 19 as 'he who shall make Order have bones'-that is, he who shall make Ahura MazdÄ's ordered world a permanent living thing (Pahlavi: the Final Body), filled with vital juices ( frasha). Together with his companions, he will raise the dead and deprive the Dark Spirit of command forever (Yasht 19.11-12, 92-96; Bundahishn 34.23).

### SUMMARY PASSAGE 9

### Gods In The Old Avesta And The Life-Giving Immortals

Throughout the 20th century, the pervasive notion among Iranists that Zarathustra, as a strict monotheist, had rejected all gods other than Ahura Mazda therefore led them to reject or explain away any possible mentions of deities in the Old Avesta. For instance, at the end of the Old Avesta, Airyaman (the Old Indic Aryaman) is invited, presumably in his function as healer of the world (Yasna 54.1): "Let speedy Airyaman come here for support for [our] men and women, for the support of Zarathustra's good thought, by which [his] *daēnā* may gain a welldeserved fee." Since the divinity of Airyaman is relatively explicit in this strophe, this was long taken as proof that the strophe was not composed by "Zarathustra himself."
