# “Building a New Vision of the Past in the Sasanian Empire: The Sanctuaries of Kayānsih and the Great Fires of Iran”\*

Matthew P. Canepa  
University of Minnesota

## Abstract

This article analyzes how Zoroastrian holy sites as celebrated in the Avesta or elaborated in later, related traditions, emerged as important architectural and ritual centers in late antiquity. Instead of ancient foundations whose details were lost in the depths of time, this paper argues that some of the holiest sanctuaries of the Zoroastrian religion, including Ādur Gušnasp, Ādur Farnbāg, Ādur Burzēn-Mihr, Ādur Karkōy and Lake Kayānsih, emerged no earlier than the Arsacid era, and were actively manipulated and augmented by the Sasanian dynasty. These ‘Avestan’ sites of memory emerged at locales with no previous Achaemenid monumental construction, but did benefit from beautiful and dramatic natural features. In late antiquity these natural features, usually mountains or lakes, took on the names and significance of the sacred geography of as found in the Avesta. The Sasanian dynasty in particular built grand monumental complexes as its sovereigns sought to take control of these ancient Iranian traditions.

## Keywords

Fire temple, *Kayanid*, Parthian, Sasanian

## Introduction

This paper deals with problems of continuity, rupture and memory in Iranian culture of late antiquity. During the Middle Iranian period the built environment played a significant role in enacting and negotiating shifts in Iranian royal ideologies as well as and conceptions of the past. New built and ritual environments played an especially important role as the Sasanians (224-642 CE) supplanted the Arsacids and broadened their focus from a localized Persian kingship to a more encompassing, pan-Iranian conception of power. Here, I analyze how some of the holiest sites of the Zoroastrian religion (as celebrated in the Avesta or elaborated in later, related traditions), emerged as important architectural and ritual centers in late antiquity. Rather than assume these sites were ancient foundations whose details were lost in the depths of time, this paper argues that the only reliable primary sources available, that is, archaeological, art historical and sigillographical, suggest that the prominence of these sites was a Middle Iranian invention. I argue that some of the holiest sanctuaries of the Zoroastrian religion, including Ādur Gušnasp, Ādur Farnbāg, Ādur Burzēn-Mihr, Ādur Karkōy and Lake Kayānsih, were invented or drastically reorganized by the Arsacids and, most notably, under the Sasanian dynasty. I explain how this revision was the result of the active manipulation on the part of those in power with respect to their cultures' sense of time, identity and the sacred, and subsequently reflects the the very contours of the Arsacids' and Sasanians' competing visions of the world.

In many ways this paper arises out of a discomfort with an insistence encountered in earlier scholarship that sites connected with the Avestan heritage, such as the Three Fires of Iran, were founded in the Achaemenid (550-330 BCE) period or earlier. Much of this has been built on the work of Mary Boyce who, modifying theories of Stig Wikander and Kurt Erdmann, posited that, under the Achaemenids a temple cult of fire developed in order to compete with an introduction of a temple cult of images, which she understood to have occurred under Artaxerxes II (Boyce 1975b, 459-65; Boyce 1982, 216-31; Boyce 1991, 74; Canepa 2013, 321). These theories grew from a fragment of the *Babyloniaka* of Berossos, a Babylonian priest who wrote a history of Babylon in Greek in the early Hellenistic era. In it, Berossos states that Artaxerxes II promoted the veneration of statues of the goddess Anāhitā, however, he never mentions temples (Burstein 1978, 5.2, 29; Canepa 2013, 321).

Boyce hypothesized that an offended orthodox Zoroastrian community must have created a rival temple cult of fire to counter this new introduction of statues and temples. She argued that Avestan sites such as the Great Fires of Iran were founded in the late Achaemenid era, when the Zoroastrian temple cult of fire began. The problem is, however, that no archaeological evidence exists that can corroborate these claims of grand Achaemenid Zoroastrian fire temples, much less architecture associated with specific sanctuaries, such

as the Great Fires (Canepa 2013). In fact, all available archaeological and primary source evidence suggests a much different pattern.

This largely comes down to a problem of the priority of sources, a perpetual challenge in the history of late antique Iranian art and archaeology as with military, religious or institutional history. In previous publications I have advocated for a modified system of priority of sources established by Phillipe Gignoux and expanded by Rika Gyselen (Canepa 2009, xvii-xviii; Canepa 2010b; Gyselen 2009; Gignoux 1984, 1979). As a corrective to distortions wrought by histories of the period based on uncritical use of Classical and Arabic sources, Gignoux advocated for a hierarchy of sources which the field of Middle Iranian studies, and I generally accept with some modifications: primary-documents written in the languages of, and at the same time as, the Sasanian Empire; secondary-documents in the same languages but post-Sasanian; tertiary-documents in all other languages. Conclusions relying only on secondary and tertiary documents should not be the basis for specific or fundamental claims and should always defer to primary sources, when possible. As a historian of art and archaeology, I have modified this approach to take into account the priority of visual and archaeological evidence, when with indisputable co-temporal and authentic material such as rock reliefs, coins, archaeologically derived structures, artifacts and seals forming the primary sources, and objects such as silver plate and silk, which lack provenance or come from post-Sasanian manufacture, forming the secondary and tertiary sources, as well. When one applies this more disciplined approach to the evidence, a much different picture emerges. The pattern of evidence suggests that these celebrated ‘Avestan’ sites neither pre-dated the Achaemenid Empire, nor emerged spontaneously after they took power. Rather, they took shape within the context of Middle Iranian, and especially Sasanian, royal patronage and strategies of legitimization.

## **Changing Conceptions of the Past and Royal Power**

The Middle Iranian period, defined on either end by the invasions of Alexander (in the late fourth century BC) and Islam (in the first half of the seventh century), went through a radical transformation and experienced a creative resurgence of Iranian culture in Western Asia and the wider Iranian world, spearheaded by the empires of the Arsacids and Sasanians. Although both the Arsacids and Sasanians upset the *status quo*, they were quick to assert that they were the rightful heirs of a long line of kings and heroes. This theme appears across a wide range of evidence, and is reflected by the propaganda intended

{3}------------------------------------------------

to quell internal dissent as they consolidated their hold over their new empire, and the diplomatic discourse with the Romans (Canepa 2009, 34-52). The Arsacids and Sasanians both integrated themselves into what is variously referred to by scholars as 'Avestan history,' 'Iranian national history' or 'Iranian sacred historiography.' This refers to the oldest myths of Iranian religion, which were shared in as many variations as Iranian peoples, Zoroastrian or not, descended from the common culture shared by the Indo-Aryan progenitors of the Iranian and Vedic peoples (Yarshater 1983, 343-477).

Parthian kings came to be celebrated for preserving the Avesta and the memory of legendary kings and heroes, and so eventually the stories of the noble Parthian families became interwoven with those of the mythical Kayānids (Av. Kauui-) (Yarshater 1983, 458-61, 474-6; Boyce 1991, 74; Wiesehöfer 1994; Wiesehöfer 1996, 133-34; Darayee 2001-2002, 6). Amplifying a process that began before the dynasty took supreme power, the first kings of the Sasanian dynasty, Ardashir I and Shāpur I, fashioned a new vision of the past soon after they overthrew the Arsacids. They created a repertoire of memory practices and calculatingly seized or destroyed all sites and traditions that could be leveraged to buttress a claim to royal power (Canepa 2010b; Huff 2008). The founders of the dynasty reconstituted and reanimated the awe-inspiring, yet half-understood, ruins of Pars, weaving together old and new sites, rituals and images at Naqsh-e Rostam and the region Persepolis (Staxr), where they created rock reliefs and founded fires that celebrated their memory. In addition, these first two kings created vital, new sites of memory such as Ardaxšīr-Xwarrah and Bay-Šābuhr. The rich topography of memory that the first two kings of kings created a tangible and powerful vision of the past one in which the Sasanian foundations and the Achaemenid remains were forged into a coherent whole. Just as importantly, their sites and techniques enabled their successors to continually reconnect with this heritage and create new, politically useful, and powerful visions of the past themselves.

Almost at the same time, during this process, the Sasanians turned to the eastern Iranian conception of the past that the Arsacids had already engaged (Daryae 2002-2003, 33-45; Daryae 2001-2002, 1-14; Daryae 1995, 140; Boyce 2001, 127-28; Boyce 1975, 84-108; Christensen 1931, 67, 105-108, 274; Yarshater 1983, 368-70; Gnoli 1989, 136-37, 178). This ongoing process gained momentum in the fourth century and, in the end, overshadowed their native Persian traditions. At this point, like the Arsacids before them, the Sasanians began to produce a genealogy that incorporated their dynasty-along with the Achaemenids-into this larger Iranian mythical tradition. Their ultimate goal was to create an all-encompassing universal history that placed them squarely in the center of an official pan-Iranian conception of power

{4}------------------------------------------------

(Pourshariati 2008, 385-86; Huyse 2008; Darayee 2001-2002, 6; Daryaei 2006; Daryaei 2007; Boyce 1991, 74; Yarshater 1983, 402-411). This increasing importance of Avestan concepts and names was not just the result of the introduction of new and foreign concepts. The Achaemenids were already influenced by the Avesta and skillfully used its concepts to bolster their royal ideology (Lincoln 1996 and 2007; Skjærvø 2005). Rather, it was the fact that the Arsacids had already incorporated this powerful tradition of kingship which was the most immediate force compelling the Sasanians to appropriate or defeat their claims. Like so many other elements of their early empire, while the Sasanians were intent upon defeating and supplanting the Arsacids' claims, those very same Arsacid strategies ended up influencing and imprinting upon the Sasanians' own activities.

If we restrict ourselves to only the evidence grounded in primary sources, in this case coins, seals and inscriptions, it appears that the first Sasanians already had begun to use the Avestan concept of (an eastern Iranian word), *xwarrah*. The earliest evidence (providing they be reliable reflections of the original names), indicating the rise of this Avestan concept, come from the names of Ardashir I's foundation *Ardaxšīr-Xwarrah*, and Shāpur II's *Erān-Bay-Šābuhr*, as attested to on seals (Gyselen 1989, 44, 46-47). An *Ardaxšīr-Xwarrah*, with the Middle Persian version of the word, appears in Shāpur I's *Ka'ba-ye Zardosht* inscription (ŠKZ 29); yet apart from toponyms, the first epigraphic attestation of this word come in Narseh's *Paikuli* inscription, where it occurs with frequency and in a context that matches the Avestan usage (Skjærvø *Paikuli* 3.1, 94; Gignoux 1972, 22; Gnoli 1999, 9: 313-14; Gignoux 2007). Narseh's *Paikuli* inscription is the first to suggest an integration of this concept, calling himself, "*Narseh ī Abzūdxwarrah*" ('Narseh whose *xwarrah* is increased'), though this phrase is not yet part of his official titulature on coins (NPi 56; Skjærvø *Paikuli* 3.2: 92).

This process further intensified under Yazdgerd I (r. 399-421 CE), with kings now fully incorporating the Kayānid dynastic title, taking the dynasty's personal names, and associated epic phrases as their own. A Sasanian king had not expanded the titulature since Shāpur I: *mazdēsn bay rāmsāhr Yazdgerd šāhān šāh ī Ērān ud Anērān* ('the Mazda worshipping Lord, who brings peace to the kingdom, Yazdgerd, king of kings of Iran and non-Iran' (Huyse 2006, 185-8). The inclusion of a Kayānid title *rāmsāhr*, and later the actual dynastic name 'Kay,' (Av. *Kauui-*), preceded the introduction of Kayānid personal names such as Khosrow (Mid. Pers. *Husraw*, Av. *Kauui Haostrauuah*), which supplemented names of western Persian derivation, such as Ardashir, that they shared with the Achaemenids. Yazdgerd I adopted the Kayānid title

{5}------------------------------------------------

*rāmšāhr* ('he who brings peace to the kingdom'), succeeding kings began to strike coins taking the dynastic title *Kay* outright, and even began including references on their coins to their 'increase of *xwarrah*,' evocative of the Avestan language which celebrated these Kayānid heroes (Daryaee 2002b, 89-95; Huyse 2006, 182-89; Daryaee 1997, 41-53). Also significant is the reign of Peroz I (r. 459-84 CE), who spent his later reign at war in the eastern Iranian world and eventually lost his life. The stunning epic tradition of the *Xwadāy-nāmag*, the Middle Persian basis for Ferdowsi's New Persian *Shāhnāma*, reflects this late Sasanian amalgamation of sacred legends and contemporary political realities (Yarshater 1983, 402-411; Daryaee 2002-2003).

## Creating and Refashioning a Monumental Avestan Topography

The geography of the younger Avesta centered on the eastern Iranian lands. While debate will continue as to the exact location of some lands mentioned in the oldest sections of the Avesta which clearly reference regions in the Oxus river valley, the Pamirs, and the Hindu Kush, corresponding roughly to present-day Afghanistan and Tajikistan, southern Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan, and northern Pakistan with a small sliver of eastern Iran (Gnoli 1980; Vogelsang 2000, 49-66; Grenet 2005). While a place such as Lake Kašaoya, (Mid. Pers. *Kayānsih*) remained located in the east, during the Middle Iranian era a select number of these toponyms began to 'migrate' to sites in the southern and western Iranian plateau, sometimes with several rival sites coexisting. These included the most important Avestan toponyms for mountains and lakes associated with central events in Kayānid royal mythology or future religious events, including Mt. Raēuuaṇt (Mid. Pers. *rēwand kōf*) and Lake Čaēčastā (Mid. Pers. \*Čēčast; Boyce and Grenet 1991, 73n 29). The bulk of the evidence indicates that many of these Avestan toponyms became newly linked to specific sites during the late Middle Iranian era (Grenet 2005, 33).

Within roughly the same time-frame, a related phenomenon emerged: grand, monumental architecture, and new, (previously unrelated), ritual traditions appear at these 'Avestan' sites. Although no evidence exists of monumental Achaemenid fire temples at these sites, in the surrounding regions or, indeed, anywhere else (Canepa 2013). More importantly, not one of the 'Avestan sites' has an archaeological record of monument construction before the Arsacid era. In other words, only in the late Arsacid era do we have the first indications that this Avestan topography became imbued with new

{6}------------------------------------------------

monumental ritual traditions, and it is only in the Sasanian era that we find ample and reliable evidence for it.

## Lake Kayānsīh

Lake Kayānsīh was one of the earliest Avestan toponyms and traditions to receive a dramatic topographical setting and architectural focus. Kayānsīh was the lake that preserved the semen of the Zoroaster (Av. *Zaraθuštra*), guarded for centuries from the demons (Yasht 13.62, 13.28; *Dēnkard* 7.8.1; Gnoli 2003). At the end of days it would impregnate a virgin who would bear the Saošyant, the savior who in turn would initiate the Renovation. In the later Pahlavi tradition, the number of saviors multiplied to three posthumous sons, including the Saošyant (Boyce 1975a, 284-85; Grenet 2008). In the commentarial tradition, the lake became integrated into the narrative of the Kayānid dynasty and the Aryan Glory (*airiianəm xʷarənahō*), and was linked with royal traditions of Lake Vouru.kaša, evident in its Middle Persian name, “Kayānsīh” (Yt. 19.66-69; Christensen 1931, 22-23; Gnoli 1967, 10-12). Described as the place where, “Mount Uši.ðam stands, surrounded by waters that run from the mountain,” the Zamyād Yašt places the lake in “Haētumant,” (which is among the Avestan toponyms we can locate with confidence) and which corresponds to the region of Helmand and is known variously as Uši.ðam and Uši.ðarəna (Grenet 2005; Gnoli 2003). With its role to be played out in the end of days and symbolizing anticipation for the Renovation, the site, as first imagined, and later when given physical form, was one of the most important elements of Zoroastrian eschatology. At a certain, and unknown point, Lake Kayānsīh was associated with the body of water known today as lake Hāmun, in the present-day province of Sistan and Baluchistan, and Mt. Uši.ðam with a rock outcrop, that during the rainy season appears as an island in the lake (Figure 1). We do not know exactly when this Avestan site was erected at Hāmun, but it certainly occurred before the Sasanians began establishing a canonical version of the Avesta, and possibly reflects an oral tradition of the Avesta that had roots in this region (Boyce and Grenet 1991, 123). The site’s primary significance revolved around its connection with the Future Savior and the Renovation, and as such appears to have been an important pilgrimage site.

The site’s dramatic mountain, known today as Kuh-e Kh’ājeḥ, preserves remnants of fortification walls, outlying fortresses, a lower city, and a monumental complex (the ‘Core Area,’) with a fire temple (Figure 2). The first building phase of the standing remains established the general orientation of the complex and its basic architectural features: a gateway that opened onto a large square courtyard, surrounded with small rooms, with a stairway to the north that led to an upper level and supported a temple. The temple's features, ground plan and subsidiary structures correspond to those of Sasanian fire temples: its central complex contained a dome supported by four piers, referred to by scholars as a *chahār tāgh* design, surrounded with an ambulatory (Huff 1990). It must be mentioned that many previous publications imply that the 'Fire Temple' on the upper level was rebuilt multiple

{9}------------------------------------------------

times. Such claims are due largely from G. Gullini's problematic survey and faulty hypotheses, based on brick sizes. Gullini never excavated the temple and simply used as reference certain features, such as the entrance porch, for an invented Parthian temple, from façades reconstructed by the Soviets at Parthian Nisa (Gullini 1964, 283; Schippmann 1971, 64-65). Similarly, the idea of two adorned stairways, leading to the upper level in the first period, which came from Herzfeld's conjecture, is now considered to be false (Herzfeld 1935, plate IX). In fact, the temple has never been excavated, and at this point there is simply no hard evidence of any sort to support that it was rebuilt; only that it received new exterior stucco decoration. Stein merely surveyed the temple and Herzfeld only dug out the remains of the fire holder (Stein 1928, 911-912; Herzfeld 1935, 66-67 and 1945, 301-2). The second major building phase added several new monumental features conforming to late Sasanian decorative and architectural forms. This phase saw the addition of two subsidiary halls opening to the east and west of the courtyard with *ayvāns*, leading to domed spaces that could have been used for congregational worship. A retaining wall was added to the north façade of the courtyard during this time as well, encasing the original façade. In addition, the interior decoration of the domed and vaulted South Gate, which was the main gate into the courtyard, underwent an extensive renovation.

S. Ghanimati reported that, in the 1990's, she had succeeded in subjecting samples from two areas of the site to C14 testing. These tests, taken from the materials in the vaulting beneath the upper terrace and tamarisk pegs, used to affix stucco sculptures to the temple, could provide an empirical basis for dating the first phase of construction and a later period of renovation. If accurate, these C14 tests would suggest that the first major building phase could have occurred no earlier than the late Parthian era and no later than the beginning of the Sasanian Empire (80-240 CE +/-50) and may point to some sort of renovation that occurred in the late Sasanian period (between 540-640 CE +/-50). A late Sasanian renovation could have been restricted to surface decoration, but very likely would have included the addition of several architectural features that match late-Sasanian rather than early Sasanian structures (Ghanimati 2001, 138-39; Ghanimati 2013).

The reported C14 dates for the later phase—if correct—lie solidly within the Sasanian era. The date range for the earlier phase, which straddles the Arsacid and early Sasanian eras, remains problematic. Since calibrated dates change and become more accurate with advances in radiocarbon calibration data, we could potentially gain a more accurate date range if we knew the Conventional Radiocarbon Age (CRA). Unfortunately, Ghanimati did not publish this C14 data according to scholarly convention. In addition to

While C14 tests can establish basic parameters, more precise dating of the complex relies on architectural comparisons and stylistic analyses of the painting and stucco. Many of the architectural features and stucco remains from the first phase parallel early Sasanian developments. In contrast, the style and divine iconography of some of the paintings associated with the first phase, find their closest parallels in Kushan Bactria. If one accepts the architectural features that have been put forth as 'Sasanian,' (string courses of masonry, use of gypsum mortar and sundried bricks, shaped stone foundations, 'keyhole' windows, and the *chahār tāgh*), then the paintings logically must also date to this era (Kawami 1987, 22; Ghanimati 2000, 144-45; *pace* Faccena 1980; Bivar 2003). The C14 tests do allow for a late Parthian date, a possibility which would then require a revision of our understanding of the development of Iranian architecture. The dating should be revisited if substantial new discoveries from the Parthian era in eastern Iran come to light, indicating that the above-mentioned architectural features anticipate Sasanian architecture. While this is an issue in need of a separate study, it also presents the intriguing scenario that the Sasanians tolerated or actively encouraged the inclusion of several Bactrian gods in the original painting program.

The Core Area contained many remnants of stucco sculpture associated with the buildings of the first phase or created in the period between the Core Area's construction and the second phase. The ornamental stucco carving corresponds closely to early Sasanian sculpture and includes repeating geometric motifs, faux engaged Doric columns and figural sculpture (Kröger 1982, 35, 74, 133, 185, 226, 247, 267). Life-size royal figures modeled in the

{11}------------------------------------------------

stucco flanked the doorway leading from the main court into the intermediate space between the court and the sanctuary. The broad shoulders, drapery, diadem streamers, and chest belt preserved on the figure to the east of the doorway, parallel the standard royal iconography of Sasanian kings in early Sasanian rock reliefs and clearly indicate continuity with early Sasanian royal sculptural schools (Herzfeld Archive neg. 1173; Kawami 1987, 18).

The site's paintings were executed in several different styles, the likely result of several different additions over the years (Kawami 1987, 26; Ghanimati 2001, 94-111; Herzfeld understood that they came from the same period; Herzfeld 1935, 73). Generally speaking, they include earlier phases, which reflect the style and divine iconography of Iranian Central Asia, and two later stages, which reflect that of the Sasanian Empire, including several figures that incorporate elements of Sasanian royal iconography (Azarpay 1981, 84; Analyzed in detail: Kawami 1987, 25-50). The majority of the painting remains come from the narthex-like vestibule between the court and below the level of the main sanctuary, called 'the Painted Gallery.' If accurate, C14 dates from the structural materials of the ceiling date the structure between 80-240 CE (+/-50), thus within the late Parthian to early Sasanian eras. The paintings here display two different variations on a Bactrian painting style, which might indicate that they come from two different phases or are simply the work of two different workshops (Kawami 1987, 25-50). Some of the figures from the first phase appear to carry iconographic elements related to Bactrian divine iconographies. These include a male figure with a trident, the main iconographic feature of Oešo/Śiva as appears regularly on the coins of the Kushans and Sasanian *kushān shāhs*, who would have been contemporary with the site, as well as two rare Bactrian votive paintings now held in the Metropolitan Museum of Art (Göbl 1984, 169-70. Cf. the painted terra cotta panels portraying Oešo/Śiva in the collection of the Metropolitan Museum of Art, acc. nos. 2000.42.3 and 2000.42.4). Next to the trident bearer stood a female figure with a mantle and some sort of vertical element that could be a wand or mace. Both were common attributes of Oešo's consort, Nana/Umā (Göbl 1984, 167-68 and 170). A male figure with a crescent moon at his brow, and a figure who wears a winged helmet and holds weapons, both match the basic iconographic elements of Kushan numismatic representations of the moon god Mao and Orlango, the Bactrian Vərəθrayna (Göbl 1984, 166 and 170). The trident, the crescent, and the winged headdress all appear as elements of royal regalia for the Kushan kings as well as the Sasanian *kushān shāhs*.

It is entirely possible that some or all of these figures could reflect the patronage of a *kushān shāh*, though the nature of the evidence does not allow

{12}------------------------------------------------

us to say this with any degree of certainty. The wall paintings are executed in, what Kawami called a modified Bactrian style, and were discovered on the north wall. These paintings include several images that appear to be kings or nobles seemingly portrayed as donor or votive images. Herzfeld interpreted a figural group which appear to be a king and queen with courtiers in procession. Unless Herzfeld invented details outright, his reconstruction likely drew on his personal examination of the fragments and reflects details that do not show up in the archival photograph (Kawami 1987, 42-44). As one would expect from such an image, this north wall of the painted gallery is near the entrance to the sanctuary itself. Among those paintings from the later stage, is a figure with symmetrical diadem streamers emanating from his head, decorated the South Gate (Kawami 1987, 26).

During the late Sasanian era stucco reliefs were added to the south façade of the fire temple. These reliefs were dated to 540-640 CE (+/-50) by C14 tests on the tamarisk pegs that supported them, apparently driven into the wall at a later stage. If accurate, this would indicate that the structure they embellished, was contemporary with or predated them. Consistent with the frequency of late Sasanian images of the king of kings at hunt with his courtiers, the western section of the south façade preserved a scene of a hunter killing a feline, observed by three horseman (Kawami 1987, 19; Ghanimati 2000, 145). Other remnants of stucco originate from this second, late period of renovation, and also include geometric panels in the South Gate, which itself was extensively remodeled during this phase.

Investigations by an Iranian team in the 1990's indicated that some sort of earlier, and architecturally unrelated phase, lay underneath the construction, dating to 80-240 CE (Ghanimati 2000, 141). When Ghanimati published her article in 2000, these results were to appear in the *Journal of Sāzmān-e Mirās-e Farhangī-e Keshvar*; however, as of now they remain unpublished and it appears that there are no current plans to do otherwise. The only details presently available regarding these investigations come from Ghanimati's 2001 dissertation and 2000 article. Ghanimati stated that a stone stairway was discovered on the east side of the Painted Gallery, which predates it. In addition, a rainstorm in 1998 caused the terrace before the South Gate to collapse, revealing that it had been built over a preexisting mud brick chamber with a flat roof (Ghanimati 2001, 139). Unfortunately, the remains discovered were not substantial enough to indicate the extent of the earlier phase. Their discovery, however, provides further empirical evidence that Gullini's hypothesis, that the site had multiple phases based on the same design, stretching back to the Achaemenid era, is completely false. Should in the future results yield an extensive complex indicative of a sanctuary, even if with a different design and

{13}------------------------------------------------

orientation, this would suggest that the Arsacids could have been responsible for initial formation of the legend of the Saošyant, and the sacred, imaginary space of Lake Kašaoya/Kayānsīh, at this dramatic site in Sistan. Indeed, in her dissertation Ghanimati speculated that this first Parthian phase could have had a similar organization around a courtyard, however, with rituals taking in the open air on the summit rather than a temple—this is pure speculation (Ghanimati 2001, 196-97). Until—and if—we are fortunate enough to gain more solid, documented, archaeological information from this site, scholarship cannot discount the possibility that the structures from the first phase of the standing remains were created in the late Parthian era, with early Sasanian paint and stucco additions.

Whether they created the complex outright or simply modified or repurposed a pre-existing site, Kuh-e Khvājeh represents one of the first major attempts of the Sasanian dynasty to take control of a powerful Avestan tradition and create a monumental focus for it. Beyond the impressive architecture, which already would put the dynasty's imprint on the site, images of royal figures, in paint and in stucco, produced dominant visual themes. These painted and sculpted royal figures comprise the most important pieces of primary source evidence, of early Sasanian strategies, which link the site and its traditions to the dynasty. In the first phase, the viewer would have encountered royal figures in procession in the Painted Gallery, and in monumental royal figures stucco as they ascended the stairway to the temple. In the later phase, viewers would have encountered a painted image of a royal figure in the South Gate and a monumental scene of a Royal Hunter on the temple's stucco revetment. Notwithstanding the fact that the remains were too fragmentary to allow them to be identified as a specific king—, even if they represented mythical or symbolic kings, the result would have been an equally powerful sight to behold.

### Sanctuaries of the Great Fires

Among the most ritually and architecturally potent—not to mention contentious—expressions of this new topography of power were the great fires of Iran. According to the commentarial tradition, the three Great Fires of Iran, Ādur Gušnasp, Ādur Farnbāg and Ādur Burzēn-Mihr, were created by Ohrmazd, “for the protection of the world” (*Bundahišn* 18.8; *Zādspram* 3.84-86; Molé 1963, 452-59). While some traditions reflect actual changes that occurred in antiquity, others were the result of discursive manipulation alone, either in antiquity, or centuries later under Zoroastrianism's subjection under

{14}------------------------------------------------

Islam (Boyce 1991, 69-77; Humbach and Ichaporia, 11-13, 20-21). The time and place of the fires' historical foundations are incredibly murky. They are attended by an often-bewildering number of conflicting traditions, including shifting locations, different founders and other aspects of relative importance. Tradition held that the fires once moved around the world, giving protection from the forces of darkness wherever needed. Only later did they settle at a permanent site, always in connection with the actions of one of the primordial *airiia* heros like Yima xšaēta, Kauui Haosrauuah or Zaraθuštra's royal patron, Wištāsp (Yima: *Bundahišn* 18.10; Wištāsp: *Bundahišn*, 18.11, 14; Kauui Haosrawah: *Bundahišn*, 18.12; *Dēnkard*, 7.1.39, 13.15; *Mēnōg ī xrad*, 2.95; *Pahl. Rivayat*, 48.42; Mas'udi, *Moruj*, 1402; Boyce 1983a; 1983b; 1983c; 1991, 72-80). Ādur Karkōy joined these fires as an esteemed, though lesser institution, but with a similar foundation story and legendary royal founder (Daryaei 2002a, 64).

Despite their perceived antiquity, their names are not mentioned at all in the Avestan texts, indicating that these fires emerged at a much later time, and that their traditions were grafted onto the Avestan past (Molé 1963, 452-59). Mary Boyce placed their foundation in the late Achaemenid era, essentially because this was where she dated the inception of a Zoroastrian temple cult of fire in general, thus placing them as early as she could within her timeline (Boyce 1975b, 459-65; Boyce 1982, 216-31; Boyce 1991, 74). Before the Middle Iranian era, there are simply no primary sources of any kind (archaeological remains, seals, inscriptions etc.) that can corroborate these late Middle Persian sources. The earliest and only source that in some way might locate a related toponym, but not necessarily the fire, is Ptolemy's second century mention of a settlement in northwest Iran named "Ouesaspē," which might correspond to the equine 'asp' portion of Gušnasp (*Geography* 6.2.12; Herzfeld 1930, 71-72; Boyce 1991, 74-75). Furthermore, no evidence of any sort can reliably attest to any of the fires' existence before the Parthian era and the Parthian evidence is only of a secondary nature: the earliest remains of textual evidence in an Iranian language that speaks of their foundation are the medieval Pahlavi commentaries. The earliest of these sources is the *Bundahišn*, a Pahlavi text based on an Avestan commentary composed sometime towards the end of the Sasanian period, but subject to several later redactions (Henning 1942, 299; Cereti 2004, 15-16). The only abundant and reliable evidence of the fires' existence as actual sanctuaries appears in the Sasanian era.<sup>1</sup>

Rather than an ancient foundation whose details are lost in the depths of time, the fires emerged from an active manipulation by those who held and exercised power with respect to their cultures' sense of time, identity and the sacred. In the foundation legends there appear several conflicting visions of the world, and the scars from several ruptures in memory, as the Sasanians supplanted the Parthians. There is some indication that Ādur Burzēn-Mihr was the oldest and most venerated fire, favored by the Arsacids during their ascendancy and located somewhere in their home province (Pourshariati 2008, 364). Although most of our evidence has been winnowed through Sasanian redaction, some aspects of its original position as the most venerated royal fire survive intact. These include its connection with an Avestan toponym, and the unquestionable prestige of its mythical founder. The 're-grounding' of Avestan toponyms and thus the sanctification of the topography was a constant phenomenon in Iranian cultural history, likely stretching back to migrant Indo-Iranian tribes, thus making the search of the 'original' site an exercise in frustration (Yarshater 1983, 352). The Arsacids are largely cut out of the *Xwadāy nāmag*'s tradition of true *airiia* kings, only appearing as an embarrassing interlude of chaos and illegitimate rule, resulting from one of the many of the evil Alexander's Ahrimanic deeds. It is not surprising to find that the sacral topography that existed to bolster their rule, was similarly downplayed or dismantled (Pourshariati 2008, 361-65; Yarshater 1983, 377-78). Although all three fires remained holy in the Sasanian period, through lavish patronage and special reverence, the Sasanian kings and their clergy increased the prestige of their newly founded sanctuaries of Ādur Gušnasp and Ādur Farnbāg over that of Ādur Burzēn-Mihr. To match Ādur Burzēn-Mihr, Ādur Farnbāg gained the first king, Yima, as its founder, a primordial connection that could compete with the antiquity, if not the holiness, of that of Ādur Burzēn-Mihr (*Bundahišn*, 18.10). In the *Bundahišn*'s version of the story, Ādur Farnbāg even usurps the place of 'Fire, son of Ahura Mazda,' in rescuing the Iranian Xvarōnah after Aži Dahāka slays Yima (cf. *Yt.*, 19.46-50; *Bundahišn*, 18.10). Interestingly, several Sasanian-era legends surrounding Ādur Farnbāg even integrate 'reinstallations,' likely in order to bolster the perceived antiquity of what was a relatively recent foundation (Schippmann 1971, 86-94; Boyce 1983b).

Although seals and onomastics corroborate the general existence of the sanctuaries in the Sasanian era (though not Achaemenid or Seleucid) era, without archaeological evidence, we cannot know with any certainty where, when, and how the actual sanctuaries of two—Burzēn-Mihr and Ādur Farnbāg developed. In contrast, the sanctuary of Ādur Gušnasp in Iranian Azerbaijan, known since the Timurid invasions as Takht-e Solaymān, is the only great fire whose site is known and which has been excavated (Henning von der Osten and Naumann 1961; Naumann et al. 1961, 28-68; Naumann et al. 1962, 633-93; Naumann et al. 1964, 1-77; Naumann et al. 1965, 619-802; Naumann Huff and Schnyder 1975, 109-204; Naumann 1977; Huff 2002). It is, thus, the only sanctuary about whose development we are able to draw any solid conclusions. The archaeological evidence from Takht-e Solaymān allows us to analyze how the architectural, visual, ritual and discursive techniques of shaping the past converged to create a new vision of the past (Figure 3).

Despite this mythological depth, all archaeological evidence indicates that Takht-e Solaymān hosted nothing more than a few scattered, short-lived, and materially poor settlements during the Achaemenid, Seleucid and Parthian periods. During the Achaemenid period, only a small settlement of mud-brick houses, sixty meters north of the lake occupied the site and in the Parthian period a simple, fortified outpost was built at the northern edge of the lake. Both were clearly unrelated to any sort of cult site, let alone one of such singular importance (Naumann Huff and Schnyder 1975, 138-42, 184; Huff 2002). The archaeological evidence indicates that in the fifth century, Takht-e Solaymān was quite suddenly transformed from an obscure and deserted place of natural beauty into a grand sanctuary. The complex did not grow organically, but, rather, was created on a pre-planned design inspired by contemporary palatial structures and fire temple design. I depart here from previous scholarship and argue that the Medieval Pahlavi sources alone cannot be trusted to document a long and detailed history of the sanctuary through the post-Achaemenid period without any primary source material to corroborate it (Boyce 1991, 71 fn17). To explain this lack of continuity Boyce speculated that the fire was installed first at the Achaemenid settlement of Ganzak and then transferred to Takht-e Solaymān, taking with it some of the place names associated with it. However, there is simply no evidence either for this hypothetical Achaemenid-era foundation in the region of Ganzak or for Achaemenid Zoroastrian fire temples in general (Canepa 2013). All literary attestations to pre-Sasanian Ganzak are entirely without mention of a cult and, more importantly, the extensive archaeological surveys of the region undertaken by the German Archaeological Institute in the last century, turned up no evidence at all of a previous sanctuary in the region (Boyce 1983b; Tardieu 1998, 249-68).

Thus from a virtual archaeological void, almost overnight, the site quickly played host to one of the largest and most lavish architectural complexes outside of Ctesiphon and soon the most important religious site in the entire empire. The original complex, though extensive, was constructed of mud bricks on stone foundations, fortified with twelve-meter-thick mud walls (Naumann Huff and Schnyder 1975, 164-68). The approximate date for the early mud-brick phase comes from several coins of Piruz (r. 457-84 CE) and a new gold coin of the Roman emperor Theodosius II (r. 408-50 CE). The site's patrons translated these structures into a more durable and impressive stone construction on the same plan over a succession of years (Huff 2002). This demonstrates not only a commitment to the site, but the continuing utility to the Sasanian dynasty of their new cultic and architectural

{20}------------------------------------------------

creation. Numismatic finds, including coins from the last years of Qobād I (499-531 CE), and the first issues of Khosrow I (531-79 CE), were discovered between the razed section of the mud-walls and the new construction, suggesting that the fifth century mud-brick version was translated into stone in the mid-sixth century. Khosrow I was likely the patron as his reign ushered in a prolonged campaign of neo-conservative renovation in many other aspects of Sasanian culture (Naumann Huff and Schnyder 1975, 165-66; Huff 2002). The design of the fortifications are similar to those at Darband in the Caucasus, again linked with sixth century construction, although one must be cautious not to circularly base one on the other (Huff 2002; Kleiss 2001, 102-106). In addition, the only other primary sources for the fire, that is seals, appear at the same time as the archaeological evidence: the fifth century CE (Gyselen 2003, 38; Kaim 2004, 323-32; Huff 2002). The earliest datable literary source that actually mentions the sanctuary's existence is Procopius in the sixth century (Procop. *Pers.* 2.24.1; Schippmann 1971, 309-25).

The site soon became an important pilgrimage destination and visitors would enter through a separate gate to the north, established by the original design, and replicated in the later rebuilding (Naumann, Huff 1975, 110-118). This gate led into and through a courtyard to the sanctuary's main *chahār tāgh* (Figure 5), which was accessible from the royal section through a separate entrance marked by an *ayvān*. The patrons provided the sanctuary with sanitary and hostel facilities to accommodate visitors. These were simple mud brick structures to the west of the northern forecourt (Huff 2002). This newly created, yet 'ancient' site evidently became quite successful, such that through the late Sasanian Empire and even after its fall, Zoroastrians held the fire and the site, "in an esteem which can hardly be comprehended..." (Ebn al-Faqih 286; trans. Gottheil 1894, 45; Boyce 1991, 75 fn 41, 79). Despite the popular devotion, as is immediately evident in the site plan, the proportion of the site reserved for and restricted to the Sasanian sovereign's use indicates that the site's vital connection to the Avestan past was created by and for the king of kings (Figure 3). Indeed, from the point of view of the design of the sanctuary, it seems that it was just as important for the pilgrims to experience the royal audience hall's close architectural connection to the cult structures, as the fire itself. Ritually, this provided the spectacle of the king of kings honoring the fire as well hence creating a symbolic monument, for them, which would serve to persist in and imprint upon their memories. Just as the sanctuary at Lake Hāmun would preserve pictorial records of the ritual enacted (or implied) at the site, a body of secondary textual sources indicates that the Sasanians wove their new creation closely into a web of royal ceremonies (Canepa 2009, 13-15).

## Conclusion

The evidence currently available indicates that sites and monumental architecture celebrating Avestan traditions emerged only after the fall of the Achaemenids. We have some indication that this process began under the Arsacids but it can only be sufficiently documented under the Sasanians. To judge from the two sites that have been located and explored, these 'Avestan' sites of memory emerged at locales with no previous Achaemenid monumental activity, but which benefited from beautiful and dramatic natural features. These natural features, most often mountains or lakes, took on the names and significance of the sacred geography of the Avesta. The Avesta never mentions the Great Fires, although the later commentarial tradition integrated them into the Avestan geography, very likely reflecting the will of those in power. This strategy rooted these new Middle Iranian institutions in the Avesta and in the legends that took place at those sites. In a parallel development, royal patrons built monumental complexes to match the natural and mythical grandeur of these sites. We have some indication that this could have begun under the Arsacids at Kuh-e Khvājeh, but the only unequivocal evidence appears under the Sasanians. These Middle Iranian architectural complexes did not depend on a connection to the physical relics of a previous dynasty, as was the

{22}------------------------------------------------

case with Seleucid or Arsacid activity at Bisotun, or the much more complex memorial practices of the early Sasanians in and around the remnants of Persepolis and Naqsh-e Rostam (Canepa 2010b). Rather, their patrons intended these new sites to appeal, in a more evocative sense, to the religious and cultural heritage of a wider spectrum of Iranian peoples, and from a more cynical point of view, to appropriate that heritage exclusively for the dynasty in power.

This dramatic change in, and growth of, royal and sacred topographies did not occur overnight, nor without royal and clerical resources or sanction. The first phase of the sanctuary of Lake Kayānsih at Kuh-e Khvājeh presents a grand sanctuary built using unified plan, which adapts many features of early Sasanian royal architecture to the site's unique topography and eastern Iranian building traditions (Kawami 1987, 20-24). Both phases of the sanctuary of Ādur Gušnasp were built and based upon a grand, pre-planned design that was directly related to royal architecture in the imperial center. They bore no mark of developing organically. While these conclusions sever certain continuities found in the literature that previous scholars have established between Zoroastrianism as practiced in the Achaemenid period and the Middle Iranian period, they also aim to establish a foundation from which we can more accurately investigate the development of these traditions and sites. Rather than demanding that we embark upon a path of reconstruction and the imagining of a putative Achaemenid fire sanctuary or Avestan topography, it allows us to assess the evidence that is available to us, and leave open as intentional gaps, what is (now) unknowable, for later discoveries or later generations. Perhaps at some point, new archaeological evidence will be uncovered that will allow us to reassess the earlier development of these sites. However, the evidence that is available to us now places the discussion of these 'Avestan' sites firmly in the Middle Iranian period and largely in late antiquity.
