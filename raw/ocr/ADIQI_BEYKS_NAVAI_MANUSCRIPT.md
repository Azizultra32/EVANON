# ŞADIQĪ BEYK'S NAVĀ'Ī MANUSCRIPT

## ABSTRACT

In this brief essay written in honor of Prof. Zeren *Tanindi*, painting attributions of an undated illustrated manuscript of Amīr `Alī-shir Navā'ī's poems are used to reveal the purpose of this manuscript as a gift from the complex-character painter, Şādiqī Beyk, to his mentor and protector, Amīr Khān Mowsillu.

## I- INTRODUCTION

A major problem of Iranian art history is the scarcity of informative texts. When one is lucky to find a reference, it is usually diluted in literary metaphors and a flowery language devoid of substance. Nonetheless, despite this dearth of information, the field has much advanced through painting attributions, which many may consider as speculative and somehow unscientific. But nothing much is left to work with, if one shies away from it. And those who have engaged in attributions have, by and large, contributed to the strengthening of the field, as they have paved the way for others to build upon their suggestions. It is thus that Zeren Tanındı's contribution in attributing two pages of a *Dīvān* of Hāfiẓ (TKS, H986) to `Abdullāh Shīrāzī,<sup>1</sup> paved the way for me to better assess the political role that the vizier Mirzā Salmān (d. 1583) had played in the aftermath of the ascension of Ismā'īl II (r. 1576-78) to the Safavid throne. It initiated a step by step process going backward in time, beginning with the 1582 *Şifāt al-`āshiqin* manuscript in the name of the vizier,<sup>2</sup> then the Topkapı *Dīvān* of Hāfiẓ dated 1581,<sup>3</sup> and finally the 1579 *Būstān* of Sa'dī with illustrations that reflected the rise of Mirzā Salmān to power.<sup>4</sup> The thread that connected these manuscripts to the vizier was the participation of two artists, namely `Abdullāh Shīrāzī and Muḥammadī, in all of them. It allowed a glimpse into the patronage of the Safavid elite outside the royal household, and on the close relationship that could exist between non-royal patrons and artists. ## II. THE MANUSCRIPT

This Navā’ī manuscript, which is now part of the EMS Collections in Houston, appeared in a London auction in 2008.<sup>6</sup> It has 87 folios (25 x 15.5cm), with text pages that have 12 lines of *nasta’liq*, arranged in two columns, and 5 full-page miniatures. Text pages have all been remargined, and there is a visible contrast between the high quality gold-sprinkled paper of the text area and the mediocre paper of the margins. All of its five miniatures were damaged because of rubbing and flaking, but also by the clumsy addition of vanished facial features with black ink. Fortunately, some of the retouching could be removed, but the flaking, which is due to mal-preparation of the painting paste, was rather deep and mostly afflicted three of the paintings. As we shall argue, this flaking can also be observed on later paintings of Sādiqī Beyk, and will serve as one of the indices for attributing three of the miniatures to him. Of the remaining two, one is attributable to Mīrzā `Alī, and the other to Muẓaffar-`Alī. They appear in the following order:

1. The hunting party (Sādiqī Beyk)
2. The king and the dervish (Muẓaffar-`Alī)
3. The *khān* pondering on his fate (Sādiqī Beyk)
4. The *khān* and the *qalandar* (Mīrzā `Alī)
5. The *khān* riding through the bazaar (Sādiqī Beyk)

If Şādiqī Beyk needed the contribution of these prestigious painters, it was probably because the manuscript was intended as a gift. Indeed, among the three aforementioned Mīrzā-Salmān manuscripts, the last one, i.e., the 1582 *Şifāt al-`āshiqīn*, was to be gifted to the vizier's protector and son in law, prince Ḥamza Mīrzā (1566-86); and thus, a third painter was invited to join the two who had previously worked for Mīrzā Salmān.<sup>7</sup> This third painter was Shaykh Muḥammad, a prominent painter of Sulṭān-Ibrāhīm Mīrzā's *Haft Aurang* (Freer??). Also, as I have argued elsewhere, the ex-Rothschild 1486 *Gulistān* copied by Sulṭān-`Alī Mashhadī was commissioned by Amīr `Alī-shīr as a gift for Sulṭān-Ḥusayn Bāyqarā (r. 1479-1506); as such, three of the most famous Herati painters were asked to contribute, one painting each.<sup>8</sup> More generally, small manuscripts (in number of folios and/or size), in the production of which a few celebrated artists participated, are likely to have been commissioned as gifts. For instance, three loose pages from a small *Mihr and Mushtarī* manuscript (22.5x14cm), with paintings attributable to Mīrzā `Alī, Muẓaffar-`Alī, and Āqā Mirak, seem to fit this criterion.<sup>9</sup> Whether the manuscript was meant as gift or not, we may never find a direct clue for it, but surely the patron who commissioned these paintings wished to take pride in the joint participation of celebrated painters in his manuscript project, no matter how small it was. By the time the Navā'ī project was conceived, the senior painter Āqā Mirak had probably passed away, and only Mīrzā `Alī and Muẓaffar-`Alī were left from the prestigious trio who had contributed to the *Mihr and Mushtarī*. But unlike the said three loose pages, our Navā'ī manuscript contains enough internal evidence to reveal it as an intended gift of Şādiqī Beyk to his mentor, the Qizilbāsh governor of Hamadān, Amīr Khān Mowsillū. This is what we shall next try to prove.

First, Ṣādiqī Beyk recounts how he had been longing to meet a master of Bihzād's lineage, until such time that Muẓaffar-`Alī became his master and tutored him in painting.<sup>11</sup> This can of course explain his access to Muẓaffar-`Alī, but does not justify why two accomplished painters accepted to be associated with an apprentice, or disciple, whose work is visibly inferior to theirs and who cannot adequately paint, as yet, facial features such as eyebrows and eyes (fig. 6). It can only be explained by the social stature of Ṣādiqī Beyk, who wasn't from the milieu of artisans but a member of the Qizilbāsh nobility, as his epithet "Beyk" may suggest. And as he himself explains, he decided to forgo the company of *sultāns* and abandon the military career of his forefathers, when he sensed an inner vocation for the art world.<sup>12</sup> Even though he had become a painter, he had a social stature, and probably enough wealth, to attract the cooperation of Mīrzā `Alī and Muẓaffar-`Alī, in the latter years of Tahmāsb's reign (r. 1524-76) when the *shāh* couldn't see well and got disinterested in painting.<sup>13</sup> In addition, he was a learned man who could compose poems in both Persian and Turkish, and therefore one with whom painters would feel proud to be associated with.

The second information relates to the subject matter of the fourth painting (*The khān and the qalandar*) that I shall argue to be by the hand of Mīrzā `Alī (fig. 1). The poem that this composition is supposed to illustrate says:

Navā'ī uses the willow tree's name in Persian, *būd-i majnūn*, to evoke Nīzāmī's story of Laylī and Majnūn, as a metaphor for love-story actors, who, like leaves and branches, are united in Springtime but separated in the Fall. In the latter state, the remaining leaf spots of the barren branch are equated to the three-dotted tattoo marks that *qalandars* had on their forearm;<sup>15</sup> supposedly, it was induced by the blue ink of Laylī's pen.<sup>16</sup> In turn, this metaphor seems to have spurred Mīrzā `Alī to pick up the *qalandar* theme to create a composition that expresses—implicitly—Şādīqī Beyk's gratitude toward his mentor. Indeed, as the chronicler Iskandar Beyk-i Munshī, recounts: After being an apprentice to Muẓaffar-`Alī, Şādīqī Beyk was disenchanted with worldly affairs, and took the life of a *qalandar* (wandering dervish), travelling from place to place, until "Amīr Khān Mowsillū, who was the Governor of Hamadān, heard about him, had him abandon his *qalandar* clothing, took him in his own service, and treated him with dignity."<sup>17</sup>

Thus, Amir `Ali-shir's reference to the *qalandar*-looking spots of the willow tree allowed Mirzā `Alī to refer to Şādīqī's resurrection from his wandering episode, by depicting him as a *qalandar* kneeling before a nobleman who is his savior, i.e., Amīr Khān Mowsillū. And since the poem emphasizes springtime as an inspiring environment for reunion, the scene is set in a blooming garden.

## IV. SITUATING THE MANUSCRIPT IN TIME

Anthony Welch has situated Şādīqī Beyk's relationship with Muẓaffar-`Alī between the year 1568 and the year of Muẓaffar-`Alī's death in 1576.<sup>18</sup> He assumed its starting point to be the year of Sultān-Ibrāhīm Mīrzā's (d. 1577) recall from Mashhad, which resulted in the disbanding of artists who had worked for him there. The verse tries to downplay the cruelty of fate and if the painter juxtaposes its meaning on a composition in which a lonely prince seems to drown his sorrows in wine, one can reasonably conclude that it must relate to the demotion of Amīr Khān in 1572. If true, the manuscript can be seen as a consolation gift from Ṣadiqī Beyk to Amīr Khān, who had rescued him from his own ill-fated wanderings. It situates the production of the manuscript at circa 1572, a date that is stylistically consistent with the 1573 *Garshāsbnāma*, in which, Ṣadiqī seems to have gained further maturity in painting.

## V. THE PAINTERS

**V.1- Mīrzā `Alī** - The most accomplished painting of the manuscript is its fourth painting, *The khān and the qalandar* (fig.1), which displays many of Mīrzā `Alī's characteristics. First and foremost is his sense of gravity, as all the figures, whether standing or sitting are in full balance, with their weights solidly anchored to the ground. Other characteristics include his penchant for depicting his personas constantly interacting with their neighbors, as well as casual and loosely bound turbans.<sup>23</sup> In terms of stylistic evolution, Mīrzā `Alī's figures tend to gain in mannerism over time, with more and more elongated necks; it culminates in his *Hawking Party* masterpiece of circa 1575 depicting a *qalandar* being affectionately caressed by a prince,<sup>24</sup> perhaps a theme that was inspired by the present manuscript. The mannerism of the Navā' painting is less pronounced than the *Hawking Party*, and more in line with a series of paintings that were added by Mīrzā `Alī to a selection of the *Būstān* of Sa`dī of circa 1565-70.<sup>25</sup>

**V.2- Muẓaffar-`Alī** – Although an accomplished painter, and stylistically very close to Mīrzā `Alī, Muẓaffar-`Alī never managed to master the laws of gravity as his counterpart did.<sup>26</sup> It is thus that in this second painting of the manuscript, *The king and the dervish* (fig. 2), which I attribute to him, the foot-runner (*shātir*) on the lower right seems to be escalading thin air. According to Anthony Welch's biography of Ṣādiqī, the latter's first mentor was a respected dervish calligrapher, Mīr Şan'ī, who not only delved into poetry but had also mastered various crafts pertaining to the arts of the book. More importantly, he was an eccentric Sufi whose passionate love for a young man had caused him to wander like a "madman" in the streets of Tabriz. Like many other Sufi masters, Mīr Şan'ī's ebullient character seems to have attracted unconditional love from his followers, and his death in 1568 may have triggered Ṣādiqī's further slide into the world of wandering dervishes.<sup>27</sup> *Navāʾī*'s poem here must thus be taken as the expression of Ṣādiqī's love and devotion for his deceased master, while Muẓaffar 'Alī's illustration seems to evoke Ṣādiqī's first encounter with Mīr Şan'ī, when he was still a young Turkic warlord; hence his depiction as a mounted nobleman in lieu of *Navāʾī*'s king.<sup>28</sup>

## VI. CONCLUSION

As speculative as some of our attributions may seem at first reading, the sum total of the elements that we have gathered explain in a coherent and complimentary manner the *raison d'être* of this manuscript, and the themes that inform its various illustrations. More importantly, they provide an intimate picture of a Safavid provincial world where artists, dervishes and warlord confront spiritual and existential questions, and interact by stimulating each other intellectually and artistically.
