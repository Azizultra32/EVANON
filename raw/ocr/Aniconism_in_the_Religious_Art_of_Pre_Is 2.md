## Aniconism in the Religious Art of Pre-Islamic Iran and Central Asia

MICHAEL SHENKAR

### Introduction

This paper offers some preliminary notes on the virtually unstudied subject of pre-Islamic Iranian aniconism.<sup>1</sup> The terms “Iran” and “Iranian world,” as used here, refer to the region from the Hindu-Kush mountains in the east to the Zagros ridge in the west and from the steppe zone of southern Russia and Ukraine in the north to the Persian Gulf in the south, which in the first millennium B.C.E. and the first millennium C.E. was mainly inhabited by Iranian-speaking peoples who shared common cultural and religious ancestry.<sup>2</sup> In studies dedicated to cultic iconography and aniconism in the Ancient Near East, the Iranian world is always absent. The research tends to focus exclusively on Mesopotamian, Israelite, Syrian, Anatolian, Aegean, Greek, Roman, and Egyptian examples, while the Iranian material remains outside the general discussion.<sup>3</sup> This is so, because the subject of aniconism has never been treated systematically by the Iranists themselves. The present article aims to compensate for this deficiency and to outline possible problems and directions for future research.

In his influential book, *No Graven Image? Israelite Aniconism in its Ancient Near Eastern Context*, T. D. N. Mettinger defines aniconism as “cults where there is no iconic representation of the deity (anthropomorphic or theriomorphic) serving as the dominant or central cultic symbol. . . .”<sup>4</sup> Further, he makes an important distinction between what he calls “material aniconism” and “empty-space aniconism”—aniconic symbolism or sacred emptiness, respectively, both of which replace a figural representation of the divinity.<sup>5</sup> In a recent article, Mettinger has also

observed that the distinction between iconic and aniconic refers only to the material symbols, while “the *mental* notions of deity nurtured by the worshipers may well be anthropomorphic, even if the cult object is aniconic.”<sup>6</sup>

This paper will adopt Mettinger’s definition of aniconism and his categorization and apply this theoretical framework to the Iranian material. I shall argue that examples of both “material aniconism” and “empty-space aniconism” may undoubtedly be found in the Iranian world together with additional categories of “semi-aniconism” and “elemental aniconism.” It will be demonstrated that the “tension” between the material aniconic symbol and the mental anthropomorphic notion of a deity is a phenomenon widely encountered in pre-Islamic Iranian cults. I shall also discuss the alleged symbolism of animal representations that are often taken to allude to Iranian divinities and address some milestones in the emergence and development of anthropomorphic imagery in the pre-Islamic Iranian world.

It is often stated that the anthropomorphic figuration of deities is not characteristic of Iranian culture.<sup>7</sup> Despite these claims, there were periods in which the principal Iranian deities were represented in a precise anthropomorphic form. This is probably true for the gods of the Scythians, various deities that appear on the Kushan coins, the original and eclectic gods of the Sogdians, the gods of the Zoroastrian Sasanian pantheon, and numerous representations of unknown deities, demons, and demi-gods that appear mainly in Sasanian and Sogdian art and are difficult to identify.

However, from the very beginning, the religious art of the Iranian peoples, particularly those of

{4}------------------------------------------------

western Iran, does demonstrate significant aniconic trends, especially when compared with the evidence from some contemporary Near Eastern cultures.<sup>8</sup>

It is also generally claimed that the roots of Iranian aniconism are evident in the earliest written sources describing Indo-Iranian cultic practices and religious rites. Indeed, the Avesta and the Rig Veda, the sacred scriptures of Zoroastrianism and Hinduism and our earliest literary sources on Indo-Iranian religion, make no mention of either idols or cultic statues; furthermore, they provide no detailed anthropomorphic descriptions of the deities of the Indo-Iranian pantheon. The Iranian goddess Anāhitā, whose appearance is described in the Avesta in vivid anthropomorphic terms, is usually cited as the sole exception that proves the rule.<sup>9</sup> Nevertheless, careful reading of the Avesta provides unambiguous indications that some other divinities were also conceived anthropomorphically, including Mithra, Aši, Haoma, Vərəθraγna, and Ahura Mazdā himself.<sup>10</sup>

One of the three principal deities in the Rig Veda, Agni, as well as additional secondary divinities, possesses certain anthropomorphic traits (like hair, teeth, mouth, beard, eyes etc.).<sup>11</sup> As in the Avesta, the deity is usually described through one or two typical traits, *pars pro toto*, while his general appearance remains obscure.

The aniconism of the Persians is a well-known *topos* in Greek and Latin sources, which make up the major part of the available literary evidence.<sup>12</sup> However, while recognizing the significance of this material, it is important to bear in mind that Classical and Christian authors knew and wrote mainly about western Iran, while the eastern Iranian peoples are scarcely referred to, due to their location on the easternmost fringes of the *oikumene*.

### Material Aniconism

The most famous manifestation of “material aniconism” among the Iranians is the worship of a warrior-god in the form of a sword thrust into the ground. Herodotus, writing about Scythian rituals, tells us that: “. . . their sacrifices to Ares are of this sort. Every district in each of the governments has a structure sacred to Ares; namely, a pile of bundles of sticks . . . On this sacred pile an ancient akinakes (short sword) of iron is set for each people: their image of Ares. They bring yearly sacrifice of sheep and goats and horses

to this akinakes, offering to these symbols even more than they do to the other gods.”<sup>13</sup>

Adoration of a sword among Scythians is also mentioned by other Classical authors.<sup>14</sup> Speaking about the Alans, who were the successors of the Scythians and the Sarmatians in the Pontic steppes, Ammianus Marcellinus notes that “Among them no temple nor sanctuary is to be seen, not even a straw-roofed hut is visible anywhere; but according to a Barbarian custom, a naked sword is fixed in the ground and they respectfully worship it as god of war and protector of the regions through which they travel.”<sup>15</sup> A similar custom is attested in the epos of the Caucasian Ossetians, the descendants of the Alans.<sup>16</sup>

An akinakes dated to the fifth century B.C.E. found thrust into the artificial fill between two kurgans at Nosaki in modern-day Ukraine has been interpreted as the sanctuary and the idol of Ares described by Herodotus.<sup>17</sup> Finds of weapons (spears, axes, daggers, and swords) thrust into the ground and walls are also attested in a number of Scythian burial sites.<sup>18</sup>

The unique stele excavated at the Scythian settlement of Ust' Al'minsk in Crimea might be further evidence for this cult in the material culture (fig. 1).<sup>19</sup> The stele, dated to the second century C.E., is approximately 3 m high and bears an image of a sword.<sup>20</sup> It is possible that this is the same non-figural representation of the Scythian god of war that is mentioned in the writings of Classical authors.<sup>21</sup>

In the context of “material aniconism” we might also mention the sole piece of evidence for the worship of standing stones or *maššeḇot* in Elymais—a region closely related to Iran throughout history. A relief carved on a stone at Tang-i Sarvak and probably dated to the beginning of the third century C.E. shows a local king worshiping before a standing stone decorated with ribbons.<sup>22</sup> This practice, apparently alien to the Iranian world, was probably introduced to Elymais by the Semitic peoples to the west, but it never gained wide popularity and acceptance among Iranians and is not attested on the Iranian plateau.<sup>23</sup>

Not only are some of these animals not explained as Zoroastrian symbols; they are, in fact, *xrafstra*—a special category of creatures, the creations of the Evil Spirit.

The interpretation of the animals in Iranian art as symbolic representations of divinities appears justified in only a few cases. For instance, a ram decorated with ribbons could indeed represent *xvarənah*, based on the evidence from *Kār-nāmag ī Ardašīr ī Pābagān*.<sup>29</sup> Another case is that of the earliest image of the goddess Nana in the Eastern Iranian world. On the coins issued by the Indo-Scythian rulers Sapadbizes and Agesiles (end of the first century B.C.E.), she is represented only by her animal, a lion, which is identified by the inscription NANAIA (fig. 2).<sup>30</sup>

It is worth noting that the first images of the god Oēšo (οησο) on the early issues of the Kushan king Wima Kadphises are aniconic and represent the god by his most recognizable symbols, a trident combined with an axe and a bull.<sup>31</sup> The anthropomorphic image of Oēšo was created during Wima Kadphises' reign, most likely by incorporating features taken from the iconography of Poseidon, Zeus, and Heracles.<sup>32</sup>

In most cases the animal symbols may be connected with totemism. Parthians, a dynasty of originally nomadic origin, introduced many elements of Eurasian steppe culture—such as costume, and social and military organization—to the Iranian plateau, and the Sasanians inherited

The conquest of the lands of the former Kushan Empire and the constant interaction with successive nomadic confederations of Chionites, Kidarites, and Ephthalites undoubtedly also contributed to the absorption of the cultural elements characteristic of the Eurasian nomads in Sasanian Iran.<sup>35</sup> Therefore, the Sasanian bestiary could also have been influenced by steppe culture and art and might even have been distantly related to the famous Scythian “animal style.” The interpretation of this phenomenon in Scythian art is highly problematic and controversial. Scholarly opinions range from totemic, magical notions connected with the hunt to representation of Scythian gods (the latter is itself obviously based on the Avesta).<sup>36</sup> D. Raevskiy regarded them as symbols of Scythian divinities rather than their representations.<sup>37</sup> Either the totemic or the symbolic interpretations seem preferable since Herodotus, in his Scythian *logos*, did not mention any connection between animals and the gods of the Scythians, as he did, for example, in Egypt.<sup>38</sup>

In this context, it is worth mentioning that the *Shāh-nāma* describes the banners of heroes as decorated with different beasts: elephants, lions, wolves, wild boars.<sup>39</sup> It is likely that these are reflections of Eurasian nomad totemism rather than any reference to Iranian deities. It also seems that the animal-headed *kulāf* worn by various dignitaries and princes in Sasanian art should be also viewed in this light.<sup>40</sup>

#### Empty-space Aniconism

An empty chariot drawn by white horses that seems to have played a prominent role in Achaemenian royal military processions may be the most significant piece of evidence regarding Iranian “empty-space aniconism” preserved in the Classical sources.<sup>41</sup> This chariot was consecrated to Zeus according to the Greek authors and to Jupiter according to Curtius Rufus; that is, almost certainly, to the principal god of the Achaemenians, Ahura Mazdā. Xenophon also mentions two other chariots participating in the royal procession, one dedicated to the Sun (perhaps the *interpretatio graeca* of the Iranian Mithra) and the second to

some unnamed deity. Curtius Rufus mentions ten additional chariots, which were probably consecrated to other deities of the Persian pantheon.<sup>42</sup>

Such aniconic representations of deities, thought to be invisibly residing in a sacred empty chariot, have Urartian and Mesopotamian parallels. In Mesopotamia, many gods, such as Anu, Bel, Ea, Enlil, Marduk, and others, are mentioned in the texts as owning chariots.<sup>43</sup> In Assyria, the chariots with the stationed statues of Aššur and Ishtar were drawn by white horses in religious processions. However, there is textual evidence for chariots being treated as deities in certain, albeit limited, circumstances.<sup>44</sup> A divine empty chariot without any cult image is depicted on the Lachish relief<sup>45</sup> and empty chariots and carts as divine objects are also attested in Urartu.<sup>46</sup>

#### Elemental Aniconism

Perhaps the most celebrated and frequently remarked upon stereotype regarding the Iranian cults is the worship of fire. The veneration of fire probably has its roots in the remote Indo-Iranian past.<sup>47</sup> From the Achaemenian period, if not earlier, fire functioned as a visual manifestation of the divine. Achaemenian kings can be seen worshipping in front of the stepped fire-altar at Naqš-e Rostam, and similar altars are indeed attested in the archaeological record, the most famous being the two monumental plinths in the Sacred Precinct at Pasargadae.<sup>48</sup>

After the Achaemenians, the fire-altar appears on the coins of the *frataraka* rulers of Pars and their successor, Ardašīr I, the founder of the Sasanian dynasty.<sup>49</sup> On most coin types of Ardašīr I, the altar is depicted in combination with lion legs, which should be perhaps interpreted as a joint image of fire-altar and divine throne, thus emphasizing the fire-altar as the seat of an invisible, aniconic divinity (fig. 3).<sup>50</sup> Ardašīr's son Šāpūr I added two figures in royal attire flanking the altar, and this composition eventually became the most popular reverse design of the Sasanian coins.<sup>51</sup>

Besides fire, other elements such as water, the sun and the moon, the earth, the wind and the

sky were considered important manifestations of divinities or even divine beings themselves. The *topos* that Persians took the natural elements to be the only possible incarnation of the gods is found in many Classical accounts and fully accords with the available Iranian sources.<sup>60</sup> This phenomenon adds another aniconic manifestation that perhaps could be labeled “elemental aniconism.”

Worship of elements and celestial bodies and even deification and anthropomorphization of natural entities such as rivers and mountains is also attested in Mesopotamia.<sup>61</sup> It is possible that Iranian aniconism and anthropomorphism owed much to the Mesopotamian heritage. Indeed, Iranian avoidance of human-shaped divinities was probably determined not only by an “aniconic nomadic heritage” but also by the incorporation of cultic practices and ideas originating in first millennium B.C.E. Mesopotamia: the substitution of human-shaped portrayals of deities by non-anthropomorphic symbols. In ancient Mesopotamia, the deities were commonly perceived to have a human form and were represented anthropomorphically.<sup>63</sup> It is important to note that the two principal gods of the Mesopotamian pantheon, Anu and Enlil, were never portrayed in human shape.<sup>64</sup> This avoidance of pictorial representation is difficult to explain, but as T. Ornan puts it, “such an absence may indicate an inherent difficulty for Mesopotamians to giving concrete form through visual images to the conceptual image of a human-shaped god . . . while deities were conceived of as personified entities, they were not always thus represented.”<sup>65</sup> About the reasons behind the replacement of cultic images with non-figural representation, she goes on to conclude that “the reasons for the transformation of the cult image into a non-anthropomorphic icon are highly conjectural, since the ancient records are not explicit about this issue. it was the awesome sacred status of Mesopotamian deities, or perhaps their sublime splendor, which prevented their visual representations from being viewed by humans.”<sup>66</sup>

It is important to note that Iranians maintained close contact with Assyrians and Babylonians for at least five hundred years before the creation of the Achaemenian Empire and their relations with the Elamites were recently described in terms of “Irano-Elamite acculturation.”<sup>67</sup> Moreover, the latest study by W. Henkelman has convincingly demonstrated that the religion of Pars in the time recorded by the Persepolis Foundation Archive was essentially an amalgam of heterogeneous Elamite and Iranian cults.<sup>68</sup>

### Some Observations on Anthropomorphism

It is impossible to discuss aniconism without also considering anthropomorphism. The depiction of deities in human form in the Iranian world is evident from the Achaemenian period on. The iconographic repertoire of Achaemenian imperial art is rich in human-shaped and theriomorphic representations and, in that sense, may indeed be labeled as a successor to the Mesopotamian and Elamite artistic traditions.<sup>69</sup> At the same time, only a few undisputed anthropomorphic renderings of deities are known. The most celebrated and common is, of course, the famous “figure in the winged disk” (fig. 10).<sup>70</sup> Scholars have proposed various identifications of this image.<sup>71</sup> However, it appears that there are not sufficient grounds for doubting that the figure in the winged disk does, in fact, represent Ahura Mazdā, the highest god of the Achaemenian kings who is constantly invoked in their royal inscriptions.<sup>72</sup>

The addition of the human torso to the winged disk, which was a popular symbol of principal deities in the Near East, was apparently an Assyrian invention created to depict their national god Aššur and in some instances also Shamash.<sup>73</sup> The Achaemenians adopted this symbol to represent their highest god, who, like Aššur, had previously lacked his own iconography.

The winged disk without the human-shaped figure also features frequently in Achaemenian glyptics. There it should be regarded as a “completely aniconic version” of the figure in the winged disk, which is an example of “semi-aniconism.” That the two symbols are basically one and the same is apparent from a cylinder seal from the famous Oxus treasure. There, the winged disk is shown twice in

{10}------------------------------------------------

the same composition—once combined with the human figure and once separate from it (fig. 11).

When Herodotus visited the Scythians in the fifth century B.C.E., he observed that they did not make images of their gods, except for Ares, whose visual manifestation was the akinakes.<sup>74</sup> The anthropomorphization of Scythian art probably began around the same time, and the majority of human-shaped representations of Scythian deities date not earlier than the second half of the fourth century B.C.E.<sup>75</sup> The influence of Greek art might have played a significant role here,<sup>76</sup> but the basic conception of at least some Scythian gods as anthropomorphic beings was probably present from the very beginning.<sup>77</sup> However, it is important to stress that the principal Scythian gods, Tabiti-Hestia and Papaios-Zeus, probably were never depicted in human shape.<sup>78</sup> The appearance of anthropomorphic iconography among Sarmatians could be dated as late as the second-first centuries B.C.E.<sup>79</sup> The Kushan kings and the Sogdian rulers in what is today Afghanistan, Northern Pakistan, and parts of Uzbekistan and Tajikistan should be credited for the final anthropomorphization of most Iranian deities. Out of 26 divinities depicted on Kushan coins in full anthropomorphic shape, at least 18 definitely have an Iranian background.<sup>80</sup> Kushan temples also housed cultic images of those deities, as is evident from the dedicatory inscriptions uncovered at the sites of two sanctuaries, at Surkh-Kotal and Rabatak.<sup>81</sup>

It is to this period, or slightly earlier, before the rise of the Kushan dynasty as the dominant force forging an empire out of the various principalities ruled by the nomadic clans, that the creation of the first anthropomorphic images of Buddha and Śiva (and other Indian deities) are also usually dated.<sup>82</sup> It seems that the rich artistic heritage of Hellenistic Bactria that was absorbed by Kushan dynastic art, as well as the influence of contemporary Roman art, determined the appearance of human-shaped Iranian gods and goddesses. The Sogdians went even further in combining Hellenistic and Kushan legacies with Sasanian and Indian influences, creating the most complete series of divine personages in the Iranian world. Unfortunately, unlike the Kushan gods, the Sogdian images are not labeled and are therefore difficult to identify.<sup>83</sup>

Nevertheless, the Greeks should not be given sole responsibility for the introduction of figurative art and cultic statues in the Iranian world.

The Babylonian priest Berossus, writing in Greek in the Hellenistic period, credited Artaxerxes II with the introduction of statues of Anāhitā into several temples in the cities of the Empire.<sup>84</sup> Curtius Rufus also mentions a chariot in a royal Achaemenian procession, adorned with statues of gods to whom he gives not Iranian, but Mesopotamian names, Ninus and Bel.<sup>85</sup>

It is obvious that the Iranians had the opportunity to observe human-shaped statues of deities among other peoples during the Achaemenian period. However, once the Persians themselves became subject to Greek rule and Hellenic art and culture became the culture of the ruling class, the anthropomorphic representation of divinities became much more diffused, familiar, and acceptable. It is uncertain how trustworthy Berossus' information is, as cultic statues in the Iranian world are attested archaeologically only from the Hellenistic period and their iconography is purely Hellenic.<sup>86</sup> However, mention should be made of the famous seal showing an Achaemenian king worshipping before the cultic image of Ishtar, but it originates in the satrapal courts of Asia Minor (fig. 12).<sup>87</sup>

The second testimony to the existence of cultic statues among Iranians is the evidence of Strabo regarding the image of the god Ōmanos that was carried in religious processions by Magi in Cappadocia.<sup>88</sup> Ōmanos is most probably the Greek rendering of the OP form \*Va(h)u-manah-, Vohu Manah, "Good Thought," one of the *Aməša Spənta*, "Bounteous Immortals," the beneficent divinities of Zoroastrianism.<sup>89</sup> The existence of a cultic statue of Vohu Manah is all the more significant, as it indicates not only that gods could be easily identified with other Near Eastern and Greek divinities but also that a typically Avestan abstract personification could be represented anthropomorphically.

Unsurprisingly, a similar practice of bearing a cultic image of a deity in processions is known in Mesopotamia. Human-shaped images were kept inside the temples, and the common people could see them on special occasions, once or twice a year.<sup>90</sup> It is important to stress that when the Mesopotamian deities were exhibited outside the temples during the year, their image was usually represented by a non-anthropomorphic icon.<sup>91</sup>

#### Anthropomorphic Mental Notions of the Divine

After surveying the artistic evidence, let us now turn to the Iranian written sources. The Avesta and the corpus of Zoroastrian Middle Persian literature composed, or at least assembled and edited, in the 9th century but for the most part reflecting earlier material, preserve clear indications of the tension between the initial Iranian anthropomorphic perception of the divine and its aniconic representations.

This tension is expressed in two important parallel notions, two modes of being found in the Zoroastrian scriptures—*mēnōg* and *gētīg*.<sup>96</sup> *Gētīg* stands for “the material, earthly (world), that which can be apprehended through the senses” while *mēnōg* is best described as “that which is non-material, non-sensual, intelligible, incorporeal and unperceived by the senses.”<sup>97</sup> The principal distinction is usually made between the visible and material characteristics of *gētīg* and the invisible and non-material ones of *mēnōg*.<sup>98</sup>

Unequivocal allusions to the anthropomorphic shape of Ahura Mazdā and the Amōša Spēnta are found in Middle Persian literature also. In the *Pahlavi Rivāyat Accompanying the Dādestān i dēnīg*, Ōhrmazd and Spandarmad are described as an affectionate, embracing couple.

When Zoroaster sat before Ohrmazd, and Wahman and Ardwahišt and Šahrewar and Hordad and Amurdad and Spandarmad are sitting around Ohrmazd, Spandarmad sits at his side, and she brought her hand (to be placed) on (his) neck. In the *Dādestān i dēnīg* itself, we find the notion that it is possible to see Ōhrmazd in *mēnōg* "through wisdom and the power of similitude."

The eighteenth question: when the soul of the righteous and that of the wicked go to the spiritual world, is it possible for them to see Ohrmazd and Ahriman or not? The reply is this: it has been said of Ahriman that he has no material existence. His form is not completely visible, but he is seen through wisdom and the power of similitude.<sup>103</sup>

Some Islamic sources also present relevant and interesting information about the Persian religion. In a passage by Al-Jayhānī quoted by Shahrastānī (d. 1153 C.E.), Ōhrmazd is said to have created the world in the following manner: "I created the whole of this world from myself. I created the souls of the righteous from the hair of my head; the sky from my brain; nails and arms (?) from my forehead; the sun from my eye; the moon from my nose; the stars from my tongue; Srōš and the rest of the angels from my ear; the earth from the tendon of my foot."<sup>104</sup>

What is evident from these texts, and also from several other places in Pahlavi literature, is that the gods were basically perceived as having human form in *mēnōg*, but their manifestations in the visible world, *gētīg*, usually assumed symbolic, metaphoric, elemental, and aniconic shape. The idea that the deities do not have an anthropomorphic representation in the physical plane, which is visible to ordinary people, may have been one of the main theoretical concepts behind the Iranian inclination to represent their divinities in anthropomorphic form. It is also clear that human-shaped images existed during certain periods in some Iranian religions, but there is usually no way of knowing whether this reflects nuances in cult or even religious beliefs.

The idea of aniconism as a "primitive" and "ancient" form of worship that preceded figural representation can be definitely put to rest. It is clear that in most cultures, cultic statues co-existed with non-figural symbols, and aniconism was just "another way to visualize the divine."<sup>105</sup>

### Conclusions

In conclusion, I want to emphasize that the cultic practices attested among Pre-Islamic Iranians were very diverse. Both "material" and "empty-space" aniconism are found, although the most frequent and common is "elemental aniconism."

The basic perception of Iranian deities was anthropomorphic. Iranian divinities were always viewed as possessing a human form in *mēnōg*, but were not always represented in this way in *gētīg*. For instance, the Sasanian cult was probably predominantly aniconic, while that of the Kushans and Sogdians included cultic images.

At this preliminary stage of research, it seems that aniconic trends were present among Ira-

nians from the very beginning, reflecting their nomadic origins, ideology, and religious beliefs, but these trends were also inspired by similar developments in Mesopotamia, Elam, and Urartu. The influence that these civilizations have had on the formation of Iranian culture has always been acknowledged,<sup>106</sup> but the tremendous extent of this influence has only recently been fully appreciated.
